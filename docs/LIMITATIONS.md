# Known limitations & roadmap

Honest list of what PhysicsLab does **not** do (yet). Nothing in the UI pretends otherwise.

## Physics

* **Rigid joints are not breakable.** The engine does not expose constraint impulses for rigid joints, so a weld/hinge/slider cannot report how loaded it is. Springs *are* breakable (their force is measured exactly) — stiff springs with a break force make breakable struts for trusses. Estimating rigid-joint loads (e.g. by instrumenting the solver or resolving per-island momentum balances) is the main item on the destruction roadmap.
* **Soft bodies** (jelly, balloon, cloth, cable, volumetric mesh, tyre) are simulated as particle lattices. They can be fastened to rigid bodies and joints (SF-1: an attachment fastens every particle inside a region rigidly to a body; rigid bodies much heavier than the soft body — more than about 40× — make the solver stretch and jitter, which the inspector warns about), and cloth and cable can be cut or torn (SF-2). Their cost grows with particle count (a 24×24 cloth is ≈ 3 ms/step; self-collisions roughly double it, so keep those for small cloths). Changing a soft body's parameters while simulating rebuilds it at its starting pose. No fluids; rigid-body fracture is not implemented (cloth and cable tearing is).
* **Soft attachments are rigid pins, not springs.** A fastened particle moves with its body; there is no per-fastening break force (a fastening is released when its linked joint breaks, the body is removed, or by command). Attachments to a body added after the soft body, or to a body rebuilt by an edit, are re-fastened at the next step; after a time-travel restore, fastenings come back exactly as recorded, but an attachment that had not been fastened yet is fastened at the particles' *current* positions (the build-time positions are not part of a snapshot).
* **Elastic energy of soft bodies counts the edge springs only** (½·k·(l − l₀)² per stretch and bend edge, k = ω²·m/2 with ω = 2π × the edge softness frequency). The engine's volume and shape-matching constraints store energy too but expose no closed form, so a compressed jelly reports less elastic energy than it holds. A stretched, undamped cable conserves kinetic + elastic energy to ~25 % over a period at 120 Hz and ~10 % at 480 Hz — the solver is implicit and dissipates slightly (tests/softSf.test.ts › SF-8).
* **Young's modulus is an effective readout, not an input of the solver.** `E ≈ ρ·(2π f·h)²/2` (ρ mean density, h particle spacing, f the stiffness in Hz) is how stiff the discretised lattice behaves in the continuum limit; it depends on the resolution, because the engine's softness is per constraint. Typing a modulus sets the Hz so that the formula holds at the current resolution. Poisson ratio and permanent set (plastic yield) are passed to the engine's material as-is; Poisson mainly matters for volumetric meshes.
* **Volumetric meshes (SF-5)** are built from a closed surface: convex shapes use their hull (a gear or any concave shape therefore becomes its convex hull until imported-mesh triangles can be passed on), the analytic primitives are sampled and hulled. The engine voxelises a cell beyond the surface, so the collision and the drawing use the exact skinned surface while the lattice reaches slightly beyond it. Tearing a volumetric mesh splits its lattice but not its skin mapping (only cloth and cable are meant to tear).
* **Pneumatic tyres need pressure and stiffness to carry load, but very stiff ones misbehave while rolling.** A 60 kg hub on a 400 Hz, 1.3× tyre keeps its 0.8 m diameter standing still, while at 40 Hz it folds flat; however, at 400 Hz a *rolling* tyre car gains speed on its own and blows up (engine artifact, reproduced with the motors off), so the defaults and the example car use 150 Hz / 1.3× (steady at the commanded speed over 12 s). The inspector warns below 100 Hz or 1.15× and offers a fix. A puncture (`leakRate`) deflates the tyre or balloon exponentially toward 15 % of its volume.
* **No per-body soft update rate.** The engine steps all soft bodies with the rigid world, so the "lower update rate" of SF-9 is delivered as an *economy mode* that disables the engine's impact-adaptive extra substeps for soft bodies (cheaper, slightly less accurate fast impacts), together with the particle budget readout and the adaptive resolution suggestion.
* **Softness is a frequency, not a modulus.** The stiffness control is the material's natural frequency: ~7 Hz is very squishy, ≈ 40 Hz and above is nearly rigid. Below ~4 Hz a jelly stops conserving volume and collapses.
* **No concave colliders.** Every collider is a primitive or a convex hull. Compound bodies (`parts`: several colliders on one rigid body, each with its own pose and material overrides) approximate concave shapes, but nothing decomposes a concave mesh into parts automatically yet. Resizing a compound body scales its parts proportionally; for a part that is rotated relative to the body a non-uniform scale is approximated (a rotated box cannot be sheared).
* **Large mass ratios across a joint chain** (a 1 kg link carrying a 1000 kg mass) converge slowly with the iterative solver. Raise *Solver iterations* in the World tab, or keep ratios moderate (the car's suspension hubs are deliberately heavy for this reason).
* **Pair material model**: friction is averaged and restitution is the maximum of the two surfaces. There is no per-pair material table or rolling resistance.
* **Determinism** holds for identical inputs on the same browser/engine build, but live edits and the variable number of steps per frame in real-time mode mean two real-time runs of the same scene are not guaranteed bit-identical (single-stepping and zero-input runs are).
* **Per-body sleep threshold is emulated.** Rapier 0.21's JavaScript bindings expose no per-body sleep thresholds, so a body with a `sleepThreshold` has the engine's own sleeping switched off and is put to sleep by the world once its linear speed and the surface speed of its rotation (|ω| × bounding radius) have stayed below the threshold for 0.5 s. A touch from an awake body wakes it as usual. The emulation judges each body on its own (the stock rule works on whole islands of touching / jointed bodies), so it is meant for free bodies and simple stacks. Without a threshold the engine's default applies, unchanged.
* **Collision groups are a 16-group / 16-bit-mask filter** (Rapier interaction groups): a body is in one group and collides with the groups set in its mask, and the other body's mask must accept it too. The ground slab is a member of group 0 and accepts every group, so a body whose mask lacks bit 0 falls through the ground. There is no per-pair material table and no per-group solver or contact-event filtering yet. The model, the sanitiser and the world support groups; there is no editor UI for them yet.
* **Mass overrides keep the shape's inertia tensor.** `massOverride` scales the density so the true mass distribution is kept; `comOverride` moves the centre of mass but keeps the principal inertia about the old centre of mass instead of re-deriving it for the new point (the mass distribution that would put the centre there is not known), the same convention as Unity's `centerOfMass`.
* **Kinematic bodies** move at the constant velocity / spin of their definition unless a mover component drives them: the `sineMover` component follows x(t) = A·sin(2πft + φ) along an axis. Editing a mover's amplitude or phase while it runs jumps the body to the new curve in one step (a moving platform's riders feel it); there are no waypoint / path movers yet.

## Shapes (package SH)

* **Round shapes made of convex parts are polyhedra.** The torus, ring, tube, hemisphere, dome, frustum and the spiral stairs' treads are built from regular N-gon rings (`segments`, `tubeSides`), and the mesh is the same polyhedron. Their mass is `density ×` the *polyhedron's* exact volume (a default torus is 3.7 % lighter than a smooth one, a 24-segment hemisphere 2.8 %); raise the segment counts to approach the smooth solid. A pipe or ring rolling on its side loses speed at every facet it rolls onto (a 32-gon at 2 m/s on 0.3 m radius slows noticeably within seconds): use a cylinder for a wheel that must roll far, or raise *Segments*. A hemisphere turned round side down does not stand on its pole but on the facet around it, tilted by half a ring step (7.5° at 24 segments).
* **Seams between the parts of a compound shape.** A small body rolling fast across a *flat face made of several coplanar parts* (the top of a ring, the rim of a pipe) is kicked slightly at the seams — about 1 cm for a 0.1 m ball at 3 m/s; the engine only repairs internal edges for static triangle meshes. The arch's deck, the stairs' treads and the beams' flanges are single cuboids and have no seams. (Chunk 2 narrowed the contact prediction distance, which removes most of the speed a ball loses at such seams — see the entry on it below.)
* **The engine's hull builder merges near-coplanar vertices of the finest dome / hemisphere rings** (32+ sides): their integrated mass can differ from `density × volume` by up to 0.3 %. Everything else matches to float precision.
* **Arch**: a block with a flat deck and a circular-arc opening (stilted semicircle, or segmental when low); there is no curved extrados (a "rainbow" arch of voussoirs) and no pointed or elliptical arch. **Stairs** are solid flights (every step reaches the ground); there are no open-riser or floating-tread variants. **Spiral stairs** have at least 6 steps per turn, at most 96 steps, and a tread's inner corners sit on a chord tangent to the column, leaving a sliver of air (at most 3.6 % of a tread, at the extreme settings) between tread corner and column.
* A **dynamic spiral stair is top-heavy** on its column (the treads reach the full radius) and tips if dropped; place it gently or make it static.
* **Round shapes of chunk 2 are polyhedra too.** The ellipsoid (`detail` 4–12: 97.7 % of the smooth volume at the default, 99 % at the top), the rounded box and rounded cylinder (`steps`, `segments`), the curved ramps (`segments`) and the wheel's tyre are convex hulls or tilings of them, and the mesh is the same polyhedron. A shape's mass is `density ×` its polyhedron's exact volume.
* **The engine's hull builder limits how fine a rounding can be.** It merges faces that meet at under about 1.35° and mis-computes the mass of hulls with vertices within 0.1–0.25 % of a polygon face (`src/model/shapes/hull3.ts`). A rounded box / cylinder whose radius is below about 0.15 % of its size therefore gets fewer facets, then a flat chamfer, then a sharp edge (and fewer sides for a very fine rim): the preview and the physics agree, but a tiny corner radius on a large body is not drawn. The ellipsoid's longest axis may be at most four times its shortest (a thinner lens crowds the hull builder), and a wheel is at most twice as wide as its diameter (use a cylinder for a roller).
* **A polygonal tyre rolls with a little rattle and loses speed.** A wheel of 32 segments rolling without slipping at 2 m/s keeps about 70 % of its distance over 4 m (every facet strikes the ground), where an exact cylinder keeps all of it. The wheel is for tumbling, ramps and decoration; vehicles should use cylinders or the vehicle wheels. More segments lose less. The same holds for prisms and the rounded shapes' polygonal rims.
* **Contact prediction distance is 2 mm (the engine's default is 2 cm).** This stops a ball from losing speed to the edges of neighbouring colliders on compound surfaces (a frictionless ball keeps 95–103 % of its speed over 32 coplanar slabs), at the price of the engine reporting a contact only when bodies are within 2 mm instead of 2 cm of touching — so a fast body that is not marked *CCD* has a little less margin against passing through a thin collider. Nothing in the unit tests or the browser tests changed behaviour; 1 mm broke an existing map test, so 2 mm is the value.
* **Gears are real rigid bodies in contact, not a gear train.** Two gears of the same module placed half the sum of their pitch diameters apart, each on a hinge and one driven by a motor at 2 rad/s, turn with the ratio −N₁/N₂ (within 3 % over several revolutions in the test), but the contact is tooth on tooth with the profile's backlash (0.04 × module of tooth thickness) and the usual limits of rigid-body contacts at high speed or load. For an exact ratio use the gearbox component.
* **Dice.** The d4's origin is the centre of its bounding box (the engine uses the true centre of mass, which is lower); a d4 shows three corner labels per face and reads the corner pointing up. Only the d6 can be rounded (`roundness` ≤ 0.25 — more would let it rest on an edge); the other dice have sharp edges. Faces are numbered 1–n (d10: 0–9, no percentile die), opposite faces add up to n + 1, and the numbers are painted with the system's bold sans-serif font. Dice fairness is that of the engine's simulation, which is not a bit-exact model of a real die (no air drag, no table-surface texture).
* **Thumbnails need WebGL** (the palette keeps its icons without it) and are stored in the browser's localStorage (about 3 KB each); a browser that blocks storage simply redraws them each session.
* **Dice are not drawn through the instanced renderer.** Bodies with a painted surface (the pips and numbers of the dice) keep one mesh each even in a pile of identical dice, because the instanced material cannot carry the ink-mixing shader; every other simple shape is pooled as usual.

## Joint types without an engine joint (package JT-A)

Pulley, rack, gear, belt / chain, winch, soft weld, screw and the limits / springs / motors of cone-twist and 6-DOF joints are solved in `src/physics/world/couplings.ts`, because the engine has no such joint. Pulley, rack, gear, belt, winch and soft weld create *no* engine constraint at all: `addJoint` gives them an inert engine rope (length 10⁶ m) that only carries the "connected bodies collide" flag, so the engine's joint queries (and anything reading the rope) see a slack rope, not the mechanism. Cone-twist uses a spherical joint, 6-DOF / universal / screw a generic joint that locks the axes they do not release. The scene format was **not bumped**: `params` is an optional sub-object that `sanitizeParams` fills with defaults, so older files load unchanged, but a build that predates these types does not know them and cannot open a file that uses them as intended.

* **One pass per step, no iteration.** Each coupling row is solved once, as an impulse, before the engine step, with this step's gravity predicted so that a *single* coupling holds a hanging mass exactly (the Atwood machine reproduces a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁ + m₂), a soft weld sags by m·g/k). Rows are not iterated against each other or against the engine's own joints and contacts, so: (1) a **chain** of coupled joints carries load incompletely — two soft welds in series sag about 1.5× their analytic deflection, five about 5× (`tests/jointExamples.test.ts` documents the band), so keep chains of soft welds short or stiff; (2) a load that only **rests on** a body of a coupling (a crate on the nut of a screw, a block on a rack) is invisible to the row — build it into the body (a compound part, as the screw-jack example does) or hang it from the coupling; (3) a coupling between a light body and a body the engine holds (a hinged spindle) lets the correction leak into the pinned body, which the engine then cancels: the gravity-loaded screw jack climbs at about 70 % of pitch × turns even with a heavy spindle (exact without gravity or with gravity across the axis), a light spindle cannot lift a load at all. Heavy driven shafts, equal masses across a gear mesh and gravity across the axis behave best.
* **Kinematic couplings, not contact.** Gear, belt / chain and rack-and-pinion enforce a velocity ratio (`ωB = ∓ratio·ωA`, `v = r·ω`) between two bodies that already turn on their own hinges / sliders; there is no tooth contact, backlash, tooth bending or tooth-skipping, and the only torque limit is the belt's slip torque (a chain uses the same mechanics as a belt). The teeth drawn on a gear are the gizmo's, not geometry. Torque and speed trade off exactly through the ratio.
* **Ropes are straight segments, not distributed ropes.** The pulley's rope runs in straight lines from each anchor to its fixed point (ideal frictionless, massless, inextensible pulley: no wheel radius, wrap, rope mass, stretch or sag, and the wheel itself is just a visual); the winch is a single rope from an anchor to an anchor with a reel speed and a load rating (beyond the rating it pays out as fast as the load pulls); a belt or chain is the ratio of its two radii, not a loop with tension on a tight and a slack side. All of them are unilateral: they pull, they never push.
* **6-DOF and cone-twist angular coordinates use the rotation vector** of the relative orientation in A's joint frame: exact for a single axis, an approximation for large simultaneous rotations about several axes (a limit on one axis can feel a slightly different angle than the inspector reads). The cone-twist swing / twist split is exact.
* **Unilateral limits are speculative.** Limits of cone-twist and 6-DOF only pull back (`λ ≤ 0`) with a position-error bias of 20 % per step, so a fast impact overshoots a little: 0.02 rad for a single limb at 4 rad/s, observed up to 0.3 rad at the end of a three-link chain where several limits act in the same step. Motors and springs on 6-DOF axes are implicit (stable at any stiffness) but also single-pass.
* **Universal joints lock the twist about the joint axis, they do not model a cross-and-yoke.** A universal joint transmits spin about `axis` and bends freely about the other two; it is a constraint on the relative twist, not a model of the cross and yokes, so the speed fluctuation of a single real Cardan joint at a large bend angle is not reproduced (the double joint of the drive-shaft example turns the output at the input speed).

## Editor

* Compound bodies exist in the document, the world and the renderer, but there is no editor UI to author their parts yet (use scene files / prefabs); no terrain/heightfield.
* **The asset store holds data, nothing draws it yet.** Meshes, textures, heightmaps, audio and blobs can be stored in a scene, referenced (`BodyEntity.meshId`), de-duplicated, saved, shared and undone, but no importer, renderer or collider consumes them yet — a body with a `meshId` is still drawn and simulated from its shape or parts. Share links carry assets too and are refused above 150 kB (the scene can still be exported as a file); assets are capped at 16 MB each, 64 MB and 256 per scene. De-duplication and garbage collection keep a scene small, but only share links are compressed (deflate): the autosave, the library and exported files are plain JSON with base64 payloads, so a scene with large assets can exceed the browser's storage quota (autosave then says so and asks you to export the scene).
* Scaling is per-shape-parameter (diameter, height, extents); a body cannot be non-uniformly scaled if its shape does not allow it (spheres, cylinders and cones stay round).
* Joint creation derives the second body's anchor from the first; for a hinge/slider the initial relative pose is the zero reference, so set up the layout before connecting.
* **Groups** carry a pivot transform and move their members; they have no scale (resizing a group is not offered) and joints between a member and an outsider stretch when the group moves. Members keep world poses, so a group's pivot does not follow its members around when they are moved individually. Rotating a group turns the world-space vectors the document stores on its members (initial and kinematic velocities, world-anchored joint ends and their axes) but not the world-space *parameters of components* (a Sine mover's axis, a Constant force in World axes): re-aim them by hand after rotating a group (a Constant force in Body axes turns with its body by itself). Group helpers are hidden while simulating. Per-object collision layers have no editor UI.
* **Lights** are editor objects with a real three.js light each; only the first 8 enabled lights light the scene and 3 cast shadows (more are drawn as helpers only). Rect-area lights cast no shadows (a three.js limitation) and light surfaces without any image-based "bounce". Lights do not take part in the simulation (no light-triggered logic yet).
* **Paths** are edited point by point (gizmo on a selected handle, or numerically in the inspector); there is no freehand drawing tool, and nothing follows a path yet — the spline, its arc-length evaluation and `ctx.path` are in place for the mover components.
* **The Cut tool (ED-41) cuts convex solids and approximates round ones.** Boxes, ramps, prisms, icosahedra and earlier chunks are cut exactly (the halves are convex hulls with a flat cap). Spheres, cylinders, capsules and cones have no vertex list, so they are sampled with a dense point cloud (160 points on a Fibonacci sphere, 48-point rings): the pieces are inscribed polyhedra, i.e. faceted and about 1 % smaller than the analytic solid. The two halves' densities are rescaled so their masses add up to exactly the original mass, and a `massOverride` is split in proportion to the halves' volumes. A piece is a `chunk` shape (corner points stored to 4 decimals in a hidden parameter, scaled by the size), its origin is its volume centroid; `comOverride` is not carried over, compound bodies, locked and hidden bodies, soft bodies, groups and lights are not cut, and a cut is only possible in edit mode (not while simulating). The spin of the original is passed on as ω × r at each centroid (an initial velocity, no fracture energy). Joints stay on the half that holds their anchor; the other half is free.
* The library lives in browser storage (a few MB). Use export/share links for anything important.

## Registries, maps and the command palette

* **Joint types are still a closed union** (`JointType`), not a registry: adding one touches seven known places (checklist in `docs/EXTENDING.md`, guarded by `tests/jointTypes.test.ts`). A new *behaviour* on a joint should be a component with `appliesTo: 'joint'`.
* **UI extensions load once at start-up**, in file-name order, after the built-ins; there is no unloading or hot swapping of a file (a code change reloads the page). A panel, tool or command registered by code at run time does appear and disappear live, and every `register…` call returns its unregister function.
* **Maps**: the `spawn` point and the `difficulty` of a map are stored, validated and tested, but nothing consumes the spawn yet (vehicles, characters and the game layer will), and the Maps tab shows a top-down *sketch* drawn from the map's document as its thumbnail until an offscreen-rendered one is plugged into `MapInfo.thumbnail`. The Maps tab has a text filter, not yet category / difficulty chips, favourites or a random-map button.
* **The "sand" of the Sandbox box map is 104 rigid cubes** (a stepped heap that piles, slides and topples), not a granular-media simulation; the engine has no particle fluid or sand solver.
* **The command palette searches commands, settings, example scenes, saved scenes, prefabs and maps** — not the objects in the current scene (use the outliner). A word of the query matches a label letter by letter ("sg" → "Snap grid size") but matches keywords and descriptions only as a whole piece of text, so it never finds a command through scattered letters of a long sentence.
* The left panel's four tab labels (Add, Prefabs, Maps, Scenes) are shown without icons: four icon + label tabs do not fit the 268 px panel.
* **At 1280 px wide the centred transport (Simulate, step, reset, speed, clock) overlaps the Edit / View / Help menus** in the top bar (File stays reachable; below 980 px the menu bar is hidden altogether). The layout is unchanged from the first release; a responsive top bar belongs to the UI package (`docs/features/18-ui.md`).

## Components and protocol v2

* **Components of an unknown type are dropped on load** (with a warning), like unknown shapes: a scene saved by a build with more components than this one loses those components rather than carrying data nobody can edit or run. Two registries must agree: a type needs both its `ComponentDef` (model) and its `ComponentRuntime` (worker); a def without a runtime is kept but inert.
* **Contact events report the *start* of contacts only** (a ball hitting the floor, a crate landing), not sliding or resting contact; use `ctx.contacts()` for what a body touches right now. The approach `speed` of a contact event is estimated from the impulse and the pair's effective mass and restitution (`J / (m_eff·(1 + e))`): exact for a central impact, approximate for off-centre or fast-spinning contacts. Events are rate-limited per pair, so a bouncing ball reports its first impact within each `pairInterval`, not every micro-bounce.
* **Sensors need a moving body on one side**: the engine reports sensor overlaps for dynamic-vs-fixed and dynamic-vs-kinematic pairs, not for a kinematic body inside a static sensor.
* **Ray casts see the world as of the last step**: a ray after bodies were added, removed or teleported first runs a zero-length update step (time does not advance and a resting scene is untouched); kinematic bodies moved during the current step are seen at their previous position until the next step. The update step re-solves the contacts once, so in a scene with moving contacts a run that casts rays right after edits is deterministic but not bit-identical to a run that does not.
* **Snapshots are exact for what the engine and the world record** (rigid bodies, joints, soft bodies, RNG, clock, components with `saveState`). Components without `saveState` start over from their parameters on restore; the mouse grab is released; pending events and the per-pair rate-limit memory of contact events are not saved (so the first impact of a pair after a restore is reported even if it had reported within `pairInterval` before the snapshot); key and axis input are supplied by the next tick. A snapshot is for this build's engine: its bytes are not a file format.
* **Component state is published only on change** and read by component id; there is no history (the telemetry package keeps series).

## Time travel, replays and ghosts (package TM)

* **Soft bodies come back from a snapshot approximately**: the engine's soft-body contact and attachment caches are not part of its serialisation, so after a restore a jelly, cloth or net can be a few millimetres (transiently up to a few centimetres) from where the uninterrupted run was. Rigid bodies, joints, components, the drive network and the RNG come back bit-exactly (`tests/timelineScenes.test.ts` checks every bundled scene; the soft scenes are held to a tolerance instead). A recording that starts mid-run therefore restores its own base state into the live world at the moment you press Record, so the live run and its replay share one starting point.
* **Replays are exact for this build only** (the engine bytes and the checkpoint hashes are not a stable file format): a replay recorded by another build may report a divergence, which is the viewer working as designed. A recording from a fresh scene (step 0) needs no engine bytes at all, only the scene and the inputs.
* **The mouse grab is part of the ring, but the soft-body grab is not**: dragging a soft particle while scrubbing back and forth releases it.
* **History has a memory budget** (Replay tab, 32–512 MB): when a long run fills it the oldest and newest snapshots stay and the ones between are thinned, so a seek into the middle of a very long run re-simulates up to a few hundred steps; the input log keeps at most 400 000 entries, after which the oldest history is forgotten.
* **Rewind speed on a slow machine is what the frame rate allows**: it plays history back at `rate × real time`, restoring a snapshot per frame, so a heavy scene rewinds in jumps rather than smoothly.
* **Ghosts show bodies that existed when the run started** (projectiles and spawned debris are not in the track); the personal best is stored in the browser (localStorage, thinned to about 1.6 MB) per scene name.
* **Share links carry small replays only** (the scene and the log are in the URL, limit about 150 kB); larger ones are saved as a file.
* **Unseeded randomness is not caught by the compiler**: a runtime that calls `Math.random()` is detected by *Check determinism* and by `tests/timeline.test.ts`, not prevented.

## Effects (package FX)

* **Trails and skid marks exist only for the ground plane** (the ground slab's top). Dust trails, tyre smoke, grinding sparks and skid marks come from the lowest point of a body touching the ground; a wheel on a ramp, a box sliding on another body or a car on a bridge leaves nothing. The slip is *estimated* from the body's pose and velocities (`v_c = v + ω × r` of the lowest point, see `render/fx/slip.ts`) rather than read from the engine's contact forces, and the lowest point of a compound body or a mesh body is approximated by its primary shape / bounding sphere.
* **Impacts come from contact *starts*** (protocol v2 reports the start of a contact only): a hard landing, a collision, a projectile hit throw their effects; sliding or rolling *between two bodies* does not (on the ground it is covered by the slip scan above). The direction of the sparks, chips and dust is a wide cone about the contact normal — the incoming velocity is not in the event.
* **The scale of an effect is the impact energy `½·J·v`**, a 0.3 J … 30 kJ logarithmic scale damped for slow hits of heavy bodies. Bodies here have real masses (a 0.4 m steel ball weighs 260 kg), so most hits in a scene full of heavy things read as "large". Material behaviour is a table of profiles per preset (`registerFxSurface`); presets nobody described are classified from their density, restitution, friction, metalness and opacity.
* **Particles are camera-facing (or ground-flat, or velocity-stretched) billboards with baked shading**, not lit by the scene's lights; the only real light is one shadowless point light for the explosion flash (not on the Low tier). They fade out near the floor so big puffs do not show a hard line there, but they intersect other geometry with a hard edge (no depth-based soft particles), and the additive glow is not a bloom pass.
* **Wind is an API, not a scene setting yet:** particles feel `app.fx.setEnvironment({ wind, windAt })`; the scene document has no wind, so by default there is none. A package that adds wind zones supplies `windAt`. Gravity is the scene's gravity vector; there is no air-density model (smoke billows in a vacuum scene too).
* **Effects live in simulation time**: they freeze with a paused simulation, slow with the time scale, and a preview fired in a paused simulation plays when it resumes. The particle budget (1,500 / 6,000 / 14,000 by tier) is shared by all effects; when it is full the newest effects replace old particles. Skid marks (700 / 2,500 / 7,000 segments) age on the same clock and fade between 40 and 70 simulated seconds.
* **Physical debris** (off by default) throws up to 6 small boxes of the broken material per hit, at most 8 / 24 / 64 alive, removed after 5–9 simulated seconds; they collide with everything except each other and never make effects themselves. They are not shaped to the break (no fracture) and they are created from frame-timed events, so a run with them on is not reproducible frame for frame. Visual chips and everything else are deterministic for the scene seed given the same sequence of events (single-stepped runs are bit-identical, `e2e/tests/fx.mjs`).

## Sound (package FX)

* **Sound starts with your first click or key press.** Browsers refuse audio before the page has been interacted with, so the engine creates nothing until then (and never logs an autoplay warning); a scene that plays without any input is silent until the first click. With sound switched off, muted, or in a background tab the audio context is suspended.
* **Mute is `Shift+M`**, not `M`: `M` has always been the Measure tool.
* **Everything is synthesised, so it is stylised, not recorded.** Impacts are modal synthesis (a handful of decaying partials plus a click and a thump per material class), engines are a pulse oscillator through a filter, rolling and wind are filtered noise. The partial ratios are textbook values (free bar, wine glass …), the engine pitch law is `rpm/60 × 3` for the combustion engine and a sub-linear curve for electric motors; none of it is calibrated against recordings. A material nobody described is classified from its density, restitution, friction, metalness and opacity.
* **Impacts come from contact *starts*** (protocol v2): a hit, a landing, a collision sound; sliding or rolling *between two bodies* does not. Rolling and sliding noise exist only for bodies on the **ground plane** (from the lowest point and the pose velocities, like the skid marks), and the contact load is the body's **weight** (`m·g`), because the engine reports no contact forces.
* **Position and scale of a hit.** Each body rings at a pitch from its **longest side** (a ball's diameter), not from its true shape or thickness; the contact's direction and the bodies' spin are not used. The sound of a hit is a function of the two materials, the sizes, the masses and the impact energy `½·J·v` (an estimate: exact for a central impact).
* **Voices are limited**: 24 impact voices, 6 rolling, 4 engines, 3 ambience, 6 interface, 36 in all; a louder newcomer steals the quietest, so in a scene with hundreds of simultaneous collisions you hear the loudest few. At most the 8 strongest contacts of a frame are voiced.
* **Engine sounds follow what a component publishes.** A motorised hinge / slider sounds from its measured speed; any component whose state has a speed (rpm, °/s, rad/s), or — for jets and thrusters — a throttle percentage, sounds as an engine, motor, rotor or jet (the kind is picked from the component's type name). A scene whose engine is a pure document value with no published state is silent.
* **Wind** follows the speed of the body the camera is following, not the camera's own motion (a free camera makes no wind), and the wind and the air bed go silent in zero gravity (read as space; the scene has no air-density setting).
* **Spatialisation is a stereo panner + distance gain + low-pass**, not the browser's HRTF: left / right and distance, no elevation or front-back cue beyond a duller sound behind the camera. Doppler is optional (off by default) and uses the camera's smoothed velocity.
* **Pitch follows the time scale** by a softened law (0.5× → 0.73, 0.1× → 0.35); the sounds are not time-stretched, so a slowed explosion is lower and the same length rather than longer.
* **The `break` sound is triggered by physics only through brittle impacts** (a glass ball dropped from height, a block of concrete hit hard); fractures and rigid-joint breakage will call it from the packages that add them. Components can play any sound with `ctx.emit('sound', …)`.
* **The first time a sound is needed it is synthesised** (a few milliseconds, spread over frames, with the common ones prepared when the context starts); on a very slow machine a brand-new sound can start a frame or two late. Rendering happens on the main thread.

## Zones, races and world bounds (package ZN)

* **A zone is a sensor, and sensors are discrete.** The engine reports an overlap after the step that created it, one or two steps after the surfaces meet (≈ 8–17 ms at 120 Hz; the first step of a body that starts inside a zone is plain physics, the zone acts from the second). Race times are therefore accurate to ≈ 2 steps (raise the step rate for tighter timing) and a body that crosses a zone *between* two steps is never noticed: keep race gates at least 0.5 m deep and fast zones deeper than `speed × step` (the inspector warns about gates thinner than 0.3 m). There is no swept test.
* **Zones detect dynamic bodies, and kinematic ones on request** (the zone's `Bodies` filter): a static body never enters a zone, and a body that is switched off is "out" of every zone. A zone needs its body to be a *ghost* (or to have sensor parts); the inspector says so.
* **Zone volumes are the body's shape**: box, sphere, cylinder, capsule, any convex-hull shape (prism, wedge, cone, icosahedron …) or a compound of sensor parts. A zone from an *imported mesh* depends on a mesh-collider shape being registered by the shapes package; the zone itself needs nothing more.
* **Vehicles are one racer only when they are a group.** "Acts on: its whole group" treats every body that shares its `parent` group as one unit (one racer, lost, respawned, teleported and launched as a whole — rigidly, so joints stay satisfied). Only the *direct* members of a group count (a vehicle built from nested groups is several units), and the group must be the vehicle: the zone cannot guess which loose bodies belong together. Without a group every body is its own racer — use the tag filter to race the chassis only.
* **Race timing starts at 0 for the scene's bodies** unless a start line (or a start / finish line) controls the clock; a body made by a spawner is timed from the moment it was created. Checkpoints are ordered by number within one lap; zones with the same number are alternatives. Positions in the standings follow laps, then checkpoints, then who got there first (no distance-along-track estimate between gates).
* **Enable / disable changes the running simulation only.** The toggle action edits the live world's copy of the target's components; the document (and the inspector's switch) keeps the authored state and Reset restores it. Editing the target body while the simulation runs sends its document state to the world again.
* **Respawn needs a pose to return to.** "Starting pose" is the authored pose and velocity of the body (a body made by a spawner returns to where it was created); "last checkpoint" is the pose the body (and its group) had when it crossed the last checkpoint, finish line or respawn point, and falls back to the start for a unit that has none. Bodies of the scene that are *removed* are switched off (hidden, no collisions) until Reset; simulation-only bodies are really deleted.
* **Soft walls are springs, not walls.** A fast enough body (above `margin × √stiffness` m/s) can pass through a soft side; they never kill and exert force on dynamic bodies only (not on soft bodies or kinematic movers). Bounds are checked by a body's *centre*, once per fixed step, and apply to dynamic bodies only.
* **A "water" edge drowns, it does not float.** The bounds' water side is a kill plane at the floor that reports the body as drowned and is drawn as a sea; buoyancy, drag and waves belong to water volumes (ZN-20), not to the bounds.
* **Zone sounds are synthesised** (ten presets: beep, chime, success, fail, coin, boing, whoosh, thud, buzz, click) with Web Audio, played by the page when it receives the `sound` event; there is no sample playback or positional audio, they use their own audio context (not the Sound engine's voice limit, spatialisation or master volume) and their own mute in the Zones panel, so `Shift+M` / the Sound panel's switches do not silence them, and a browser that has not seen a user gesture stays silent.
* **There are 15 zone actions and no registry for new ones yet**: an action is one entry in `ZONE_ACTIONS` (`src/model/zones.ts`), its fields in `src/model/components/zone.ts` and a case in `fire` (`src/physics/zones/actions.ts`). Behaviours that are not "something happens to the bodies that enter" (fields, fluids, surfaces) are components of their own.

## Powered parts (package CP: engines, motors, actuators, drive train, thrusters, rotors)

* **The engine clamps how fast a body can spin**: Rapier limits a rigid body's angular speed to π/4 rad per integration step, 94 rad/s (≈ 900 rpm) at the default 120 Hz. A *physical* wheel, hub or hinge driven faster than that is capped (a 0.3 m wheel tops out near 28 m/s). Flywheels, gearbox shafts and propeller / rotor speeds are virtual shafts and have no such limit (the propeller scene runs at 3000 rpm); raising the physics rate raises the cap in proportion.
* **Drive-train shafts are scoped to a jointed assembly.** A shaft name is private to the bodies connected by joints (a static frame counts), so two copies of a car each have their own "crank"; a name starting with `@` is shared by the whole scene. Two parts on bodies that are not connected by a joint do not see each other's shafts unless they use an `@` name. The network sizes its impulses from the inertia of the bodies at the ends of a shaft (parallel-axis theorem for a hinge), not from the whole mechanism behind them, so parts that move a long linkage (servo, stepper, linear actuator, hydraulic cylinder) use the engine's own joint motor instead — which sees the whole mechanism and is stable at any gain, but whose force the engine cannot report back.
* **Servo / stepper / actuator / cylinder torque and force gauges are estimates** (gain × (commanded − actual speed), clamped to the limit). The limit itself (torque, force, pressure × area) is enforced exactly by the engine. A joint's built-in motor and a powered component on the same joint fight each other; the inspector warns and the joint's motor mode should be Off.
* **Closed linkages with a light link against a heavy load converge slowly** (the iterative solver, as in "Large mass ratios" above): the hydraulic crane's linkage uses deliberately heavy links (60 / 40 kg). A stepper driving a heavy table settles slowly by design (its damping floor is `k·dt`).
* **The hydraulic cylinder is a force / flow model, not a fluid circuit**: force = pressure × area (bore minus rod area on the retract stroke), speed ≤ flow ÷ area, a relief valve at the set pressure, a centred valve holds the load. No accumulators, shared circuits, compressibility, leakage or heating; the valve is a key or an axis.
* **Engines and gearboxes are mechanical models only**: a torque curve (editable), flywheel inertia, idle governor, rev limiter and a throttle response lag — no thermal model, fuel consumption, starter or stall. The automatic gearbox is a two-threshold rpm shifter without a torque converter. Differentials are open, locked or limited-slip (a clutch pack that resists a speed difference); there are no Torsen or viscous types. The clutch is a friction pair with a pedal, an optional centrifugal engagement and a locked state when it can carry the torque.
* **Propeller and rotor aerodynamics are coefficient models**: propeller thrust C_T·ρ·n²·D⁴ fading with the advance ratio, torque C_Q·ρ·n²·D⁵; rotor lift C_T·collective·ρ·πR²·(ΩR)² with a drag torque that is 30 % profile (always there) and 70 % induced (grows with the collective). The cyclic leans the thrust vector (and, with the hub above the centre of mass, turns the body). There is no blade flapping, ground effect, wind, vortex ring state or gyroscopic precession, and the air density is a constant (a parameter). The helicopter scene is flyable by keys because of its trim (`trimRoll`) and angular damping, not because it is stabilised.
* **Fuel is mass at the centre of mass.** Burning fuel lowers the body's mass and scales its inertia in proportion; the centre of mass does not move as the tank empties. A rocket has no aerodynamic stabilisation other than its angular damping, so a small wobble from the pad stays as a slow drift (the launch scene sets an angular damping of 1.5; its `airDrag` is 0 so the climb is the rocket equation alone).
* **Flames, blade blur and gauges are drawn, not simulated**: `src/render/componentFx.ts` draws a teardrop flame per thruster / jet, translucent blade discs for propellers and rotors, and dial / stroke / brake-ring gauges from the numbers the components publish. There are no exhaust particles or smoke yet (the `ignite` / `burnout` events and the published thrust are the hook); the sound engine (package FX) already voices them, from the published rpm / thrust and an ignition sound.
* **The Controls panel drives analog axes only with its own sliders** (no gamepad or script input yet; the protocol accepts any `setAxis`). It lists the keys and axes found in the schemas of the enabled components of the scene, so a binding that a component reads through a script or a custom channel is not listed.

## Charts (UI-30 / 31 / 32)

* **Samples are per rendered frame**, at most 60 per simulated second (a chart's rate and record length are settable), so a spectrum can resolve frequencies up to half the sampling rate (30 Hz at best, less when the browser renders slower than the simulation) and fast contact transients are undersampled. Irregular frame spacing is resampled linearly onto a uniform grid before the FFT.
* **Acceleration is the backward difference of the sampled velocity**, not an instantaneous value: it includes gravity and contact forces and is noisy at impacts.
* **Angular momentum uses an effective moment of inertia** `2·KE_rot/ω²` recovered from the body's rotational kinetic energy, because the worker does not send the inertia tensor: exact for rotation about a principal axis, an approximation for a tumbling asymmetric body.
* **Contacts are world-level.** The contact point count and total contact force come from the contact-point buffer of awake dynamic bodies (the same data as the Contacts overlay, which a chart switches on while it needs it); there is no per-body contact count or force, and no force for resting contacts of sleeping bodies.
* **No hinge torque, temperature or sensor signals.** The engine's joint readback reports angle / speed (hinge, slider), length / force (spring) and tension (rope) only; there is no temperature simulation, and sensor events are events, not continuous values.
* **Power is F·v** of the body's average net force over the last frame (the rate of change of its translational kinetic energy); use the Derivative option on an energy trace for dE/dt of anything else.
* Chart data is not saved with the scene; configurations are saved per browser (localStorage), not per scene, and refer to objects by name.

## Rendering & platform

* Targets desktop browsers with WebGL 2 and WebAssembly; touch navigation works (orbit/zoom) but editing is mouse- and keyboard-centric.
* Very large scenes (several thousand bodies) are limited by per-body draw calls, not by the physics worker; instanced rendering of identical shapes is a straightforward next step.
* Shadows cover a camera-following frustum, so distant objects are unshadowed.

## Outliner

* **Row thumbnails are real renders only for rigid bodies** (one shared off-screen three.js renderer, cached by shape + material, rendered a few per frame). Groups, lights, paths, joints and soft bodies have no stand-alone mesh in the outliner, so they show their type icon; without WebGL every row falls back to the icon.
* **Solo works by hiding**: soloing marks everything unrelated as hidden (`soloHid`) rather than being a separate render mode, so objects added while a solo is active stay visible until the solo is cleared; an object the user un-hides by hand during a solo is not hidden again.
* **Reordering changes the document order** and, like any wholesale reorder, is refused while the simulation is running (it would rebuild the world).

## Desktop packaging and PWA install (package DT-B)
* The Electron wrapper in `desktop/` was built and smoke-tested on Linux x64 only; Windows/macOS packages, installers, code signing and auto-update are not provided or verified. Tauri was not attempted (toolchain not verifiable here).
* The browser install dialogs, Add to Dock, OS file association (`file_handlers`) and the Web Share Target sheet cannot be driven headless; only their building blocks (manifest, launch-queue/share intake, service worker) are tested. File share targets work on Android/ChromeOS/recent Windows only; Safari and Firefox desktop have no install prompt, so the in-app Install entry stays hidden there.
* An open tab of an older version can lose lazily loaded chunks after the new service worker deletes the old cache; the update banner asks the user to reload.

## Inspector upgrades, calculators and readout (UI-10..12)

* Reset-to-default, copy / paste of properties and property presets act on rigid bodies; other entity kinds keep unit toggles, expressions, label scrubbing and the filter.
* Unit choices are remembered per row label (all fields with the same label, e.g. "Position", switch together) and per browser, not per document.
* The readout lists impacts (contacts that just started, with impulse and approach speed) rather than every resting contact: the engine reports contact events per pair, not a persistent manifold per body. Acceleration is the average net force / mass of the last frame.
* The moment-of-inertia readout is exact for box, sphere / ellipsoid and cylinder; compound bodies and other shapes use the bounding box and say so. The terminal-velocity calculator writes the engine's quadratic air drag (F = c·|v|·v); the hinge pendulum calculator is a point-mass approximation.

## Visuals (VZ-A)
* Triplanar mapping applies to the colour texture only; normal maps are turned off while it is on.
* Height fog is exponential, integrated analytically along the view ray per fragment (no volumetric scattering); it needs the patched shader chunks in `src/render/fogChunks.ts`.
* The "water" ground type is a visual surface (reflective, animated normal map); it does not buoy bodies.
* Physical-sky lighting is re-baked at most twice a second while the day cycle runs.
## Share links v2 (DT-20)

The compact share link stores non-integer numbers with 6 significant digits and renames entity ids to `e0`, `e1` … when opened, so a scene opened from a link is physically equivalent to the original but not bit-identical (chaotic scenes can diverge over a long run). Use *Export file* for exact transfer. Old `#scene=` (v1) links keep working.

## Pin tool and soft particles (ED-43)
The pin tool nails rigid bodies to the world with a hinge, ball or fixed joint, also while the simulation runs. A single soft-body particle cannot be pinned at run time: the engine fixes pinned particles when the soft body is built, so a live pin would mean rebuilding (and resetting) the body. Soft bodies are pinned with the Pin presets in their inspector; the tool says so when you click one.
## DT-23 — peer-to-peer collaboration

* **No server, no TURN.** WebRTC runs with no STUN/TURN servers (nothing third-party is contacted), so a direct connection only forms when the two computers can reach each other (same LAN, or a reachable address). Behind symmetric NATs/strict firewalls it fails; there is no relay fallback.
* **Manual signalling.** The two connection codes (offer, answer) are exchanged by copy/paste, one invitation per guest; a lost connection must be re-established the same way (no auto-reconnect). Same-device tabs use BroadcastChannel and need no codes.
* **Guests re-simulate.** The host broadcasts play/pause/reset and guests run the same deterministic engine on the same document; no poses are streamed, so interactions that are not in the document (mouse-grab, key input, projectiles) are not mirrored and runs can drift over long times.
* **Assets (meshes, textures) are not synchronised**; entities referring to an asset the receiver lacks lose that reference.
* **Undo is local** and not collaborative (see `docs/COLLAB.md`). Conflicts are per-field last-writer-wins.
* Joining replaces the guest's scene. Each remote batch re-sanitises the whole document, so very large shared scenes feel heavy.

## Weather (VZ-23)

* Rain, snow, leaves and dust are camera-following instanced billboards: they fall through everything (no occlusion under roofs or bodies, no splashes or ripples, nothing settles on the ground), and clip at the ground plane y = 0.
* The "puddle-wet look" is lowered roughness plus a slightly darker colour on the ground and on standard materials, swept about every 0.75 s; there are no actual puddles or extra reflections. Custom shader materials and skins with their own materials are not wetted.
* Lightning is a capped screen flash plus brighter particles, not a real light: it does not light the scene or cast shadows, and there is no thunder sound. With "reduce motion" the flash is dimmed to 25 %.
* The scene wind is stored in the weather block (the project had none); it drives weather particles and the effects package's particles, and — through bodies' quadratic drag coefficient (JT-32) — it is the air their drag is measured against; it does not push bodies that have no drag coefficient (use a constant-force component for that).

## Joint load estimation, layers and platforms (JT-B)

- **Joint loads are an estimate.** Rapier exposes no impulses for rigid joints, so the force and torque a joint transmits come from a momentum balance on the structure beyond it (`jointLoad.ts`). It is exact for joints that are bridges of the joint graph with an unsupported side, a shared-load heuristic otherwise (joints in loops, structures fixed to the world at several places). Only *normal* contact impulses enter the balance: friction that holds up or drags a jointed assembly is not seen. Springs are known forces; engine-external couplings (JT-A) are inside the measured Δv but share their wrench like loop joints. The estimate comes from the last engine sub-step, so a breakage threshold reacts to impact peaks of one step (about 1/(Hz·substeps) s).
- **One-way platforms** use a contact hook that decides from the other body's centre of mass and velocity (solid only when it is on the solid side and not moving away faster than 0.35 m/s). A very large body whose centre is behind a platform while most of it is in front passes through; thin fast bodies rely on CCD as usual. The engine borrows its body set while it calls the hook, so poses are copied once per step for every body while any platform exists.
- **Trigger layers** turn a layer's bodies into sensors (pass-through, overlap events). Switching a body's layer or a layer's trigger flag on a running world takes effect on the next step; sensors are not solid, so bodies already resting on a body whose layer becomes a trigger fall.
- **Wind** (the weather block's scene wind, horizontal) only acts through quadratic drag (`c·|v − wind|·(v − wind)`), not through linear damping or on bodies without a drag coefficient.
## DT-31 plugins: no plugin-defined components or shapes
Plugins can register commands, declarative panels and example scenes (templates). Components run inside the physics worker each step and shapes construct Rapier colliders; exposing either to untrusted plugin code would defeat the sandbox (no network/DOM/storage, killable). Scene scripts and plugin templates build scenes from the existing shapes and components instead.

## Render quality (VZ-B)

* **LOD** covers the round primitives (sphere, cylinder, cone, capsule and shapes built from them): other geometry, convex hulls and imported meshes have one level; there is no mesh decimation.
* **Shadows** are one sun shadow map that follows the orbit target with light-space texel snapping and a tier-dependent frustum, not cascades; there is no PCSS-style contact hardening (three.js r186 samples shadow maps through hardware comparison samplers). High and Ultra tighten the frustum for sharper nearby shadows.
* **Motion blur** uses a half-resolution velocity buffer: camera motion and body translation, not spin or deformation; soft bodies do not blur.
* **Screen-space reflections** reflect only what is on screen, and only glossy / metallic bodies and skin meshes (three.js SSRPass); the ground does not reflect.
* **Light shafts** are screen-space: the sun only produces shafts when it is in the frame (the orbit camera cannot look that high), spotlights always (up to three, nearest-first scan twice a second).
* **Damage visuals** are procedural (cracks / scorch / dent-tint in the body shader) and accumulate from approach speed per material kind; skinned bodies and compound bodies' parts share the body's damage but skins are not damaged.
* **Auto quality** judges by frame time on the main thread: a slow *simulation* step lowers quality too.
* Themes (VZ-50): colour literals remain in other packages' canvases and stylesheets (`src/app/camera/minimap.ts`, `src/ui/timeTravel/timelinePanel.ts`, `src/ui/diag/panels.ts`, `cm.css`, `tm.css`, `diag.css`); they keep their own look in every theme until migrated to the `--canvas-*` / `--chart-*` tokens. The 3D viewport background is scene-driven.
* Procedural pattern textures are cached per (pattern, colours, bump); dragging a colour slider adds cache entries that live until reload.
## DT-42: first load is not under 1.2 MB gzip
The Rapier WebAssembly binary alone is about 1.15 MB gzip (served as a separate cached asset), so the cold-start download is about 1.9 MB gzip; the JavaScript part is well below the target. Numbers and the lazy-loading policy are in docs/PERFORMANCE.md; `npm run size` enforces budgets in CI.

## Export (DT-1)

* **Soft bodies are not exported** to glTF / OBJ / STL: they are simulated particles with a surface rebuilt every frame, and the exporters work from the document. The export reports them in a toast and leaves them out.
* **Baked glTF animation is a real-time capture** of the running simulation (~30 samples per second for the chosen number of seconds), not a deterministic replay of the time-travel recording, so two exports of a chaotic scene can differ slightly and the clip length follows the wall clock.
* **Not representable**: area lights, paths, joints, render-only skins and texture maps are not written. OBJ has no hierarchy or lights; STL is geometry only (one merged solid, optionally in millimetres and Z-up).
* **Screenshots are not anti-aliased above 2560 px** (the viewport renderer has no MSAA; smaller images are rendered at 2x and averaged). Transparent PNGs recover alpha by rendering twice (over black and white), so the ground shadow is kept but selection outlines and ambient occlusion are not drawn. Maximum size is 3840 px per side and the graphics card's texture limit.


## Video, GIF and turntable capture (DT-2)

* **Video is real-time.** WebM/MP4 come from `MediaRecorder`, which stamps frames with the wall clock. Deterministic mode makes the *pictures* reproducible (fixed physics steps per frame, scripted camera) and holds each frame for its nominal duration, but a machine too slow to render a frame within 1/fps stretches the video. The GIF path has exact frame delays and is the one whose whole output is independent of machine speed.
* **Codecs depend on the browser.** The Record dialog offers only containers for which `MediaRecorder.isTypeSupported` says yes (Chromium: WebM, recent versions MP4/H.264; Firefox: WebM only; Safari: MP4). Where none is available the dialog says so and GIF still works. We ship no video encoder.
* **GIF limits.** At most 960 px wide, 30 fps, 30 s and 600 frames; 256 colours per frame (median-cut, no dithering), so gradients band. A real-time GIF buffers its frames as RGBA (capped at 256 MB); deterministic GIFs are encoded frame by frame.
* **Deterministic capture covers the physics and the camera, not every effect.** Particle effects, motion trails and sound use the wall-clock frame time of the render loop, so a scene with active effects can differ slightly between two captures; the physics state at every frame is identical. Tab visibility matters: a hidden tab stops `requestAnimationFrame`, and the capture then stops with an explanation.
* Deterministic capture with *Restart simulation* rebuilds the simulation from the scene, so the state a paused run had reached is not preserved (the simulation returns to running/edit as before the capture, but not to the same moment).

## Embed viewer and share dialog (DT-21, DT-22)

* The viewer has no `bg=transparent|light|dark` option: the renderer draws an opaque sky and has no alpha buffer, so a transparent or light background would need renderer changes. The viewer always looks like the editor's viewport.
* An embed carries its scene in the link, so it is limited to the share-link size (about 32 kB); larger scenes cannot be embedded this way.
* QR codes hold at most 2953 bytes (2331 at error correction M); longer links are shown as text with the file alternative. Scanning a QR code for a long link (above roughly 1 kB) needs a good camera and a large code.
* The preview card (Open Graph) needs a host: the scene is in the URL fragment, which crawlers never see, so a static deployment cannot render per-scene previews by itself. The dialog produces the thumbnail and the tags; `docs/EMBED.md` explains the landing page.
* "Copy image + link" depends on the browser's async clipboard (`ClipboardItem` with `image/png`); where it is missing or refused, only the link is copied and the toast says so.
* The viewer's message API without an `origin=` allow-list accepts commands from any parent page (it can play, pause and load scenes, nothing more).

## Data platform (DT-A)

* **Folder sync** needs the File System Access API (Chromium browsers). It polls every 3 s for external changes (no file-watcher API exists); the native picker/permission flow is not covered by an automated browser test.
* **Crash detection** relies on a localStorage heartbeat; a crash is not noticed while the tab is in the background, and a second tab with a fresh heartbeat is treated as "not crashed".
* **Restore points** store whole serialized scenes (no delta storage); the default 64 MB cap keeps the oldest automatic ones out.
* **Table export** covers what registers a data source (the two telemetry charts); other panels adopt it by calling `registerDataSource`.

## Presentation mode and quality of life (UI-41, UI-42)
* **Drag and drop imports scene files only.** Meshes, images, audio, replays and prefab packs have no importer on this branch, so dropping one shows an explicit "… aren't supported yet" message (and the overlay says so while dragging) instead of pretending. `registerDropHandler(kind, handler)` in `src/ui/qol/dropHandlers.ts` is where another package plugs one in.
* **Open recent is a headed block in the File menu**, not a nested submenu: the menu model has no nesting. At most 8 scenes are listed there (12 are remembered; the Scenes tab shows the newest 5).
* **Recent scenes opened from a file or the clipboard keep their JSON only while it fits a small budget** (4 scenes, 700k characters in total, 300k each). A larger scene is listed but must be imported again to reopen it. Everything is per browser (localStorage).
* **Thumbnails** are small JPEGs of the viewport (160 × 90), taken after a scene loads, saved or is edited; an example scene has none until it has been opened once. A browser with full storage shows no thumbnails.
* **Hi-res capture** is limited by the GPU: the scale is reduced (and the saved-image toast says so) when the picture would exceed the largest renderable edge (at most 8192 px) or 36 megapixels. HTML overlays (body labels, measurements, quick bars) are not part of the image; the caption is drawn in separately. Software GL renders a 4× capture slowly.
* **Reading the clipboard from the "Paste scene from clipboard" command** needs the browser's clipboard permission; when it is refused the command says so and Ctrl+V still works. Ctrl+V takes a scene over only when the clipboard text is a PhysicsLab scene; entity copy / paste is unchanged.
* **Lighting presets change the scene** (they are the VZ environment presets, saved with the scene; Undo restores them); depth of field presets change the graphics preferences, not the scene.


## Cameras and input (CM-A)

* **Bookmark keys.** Plain `1`…`9` already add shapes, and `Ctrl+1`…`9` is reserved by browsers (tab switching), so bookmarks are recalled with `Shift+1`…`9` and saved with `Ctrl+Shift+1`…`9`. Everything is rebindable in Settings › Keyboard.
* **Split-screen panes and picture-in-picture windows** are rendered by the viewport's own renderer into off-screen targets and read back into 2D canvases: they have no outline, ambient occlusion, depth of field or anti-aliasing, run at most at the display rate (every second frame with three or more views) and cost one extra scene render each. Only the main view is interactive (picking, gizmo, camera control); the other panes follow their own cameras. At most 3 split panes and 4 picture-in-picture windows.
* **Orthographic views** switch the perspective-only passes off (ambient occlusion, depth of field, screen-space reflections, light shafts, motion blur); the orthographic camera sits 120 m from its target, so geometry nearer than the near plane (0.05 m) to that point is not clipped but a scene taller than ~2 km would be.
* **Body cameras** (first person, third person, chase, mounted) need to know which local axis of a body is "forward": the setting defaults to +X (the car and vehicle templates point along +X) and is changeable per browser in the Camera panel, not per body. Third-person arm collision tests the visible body meshes and the ground plane, not soft bodies.
* **Fly mode owns its keys** (W A S D Q E, Shift): while it runs they are not sent to the simulation, so a car cannot be driven with the keyboard while flying — use another camera mode, or the gamepad / touch controls.
* **Cinematic recording** writes the viewport canvas as WebM through `MediaRecorder` in real time (so frame pacing follows the machine, not the shot's duration); the recorder registry (`registerShotRecorder`) is the hook for a deterministic frame-by-frame exporter. The view cube is hidden while recording. Shots follow a path's spline in world space; a path that is moved while a shot plays moves the shot.
* **Gamepads** are read through the browser's Gamepad API: browsers hide the pad until a button is pressed once, only the first connected pad is used, and non-"standard" devices fall back to axes 0–3 for the sticks and 4/5 (or buttons 6/7) for the triggers. Automated tests use a stubbed `getGamepads`, not hardware.
* **Axis-driven vehicles.** The bundled scenes with analog axes are the car, the powered wheel and the pistons; the plane, tank and crane profiles map a pad onto their axis names (`pitch`/`roll`/`yaw`/`throttle`, `left`/`right`, `slew`/`trolley`/`lift`/`hoist`), and any motor or thruster whose axis name matches is driven, but there is no bundled plane, tank or crane scene yet.
* **Path handles (ED-23).** Path control points have translate handles and the whole path has one tension value; per-point tangent handles or per-point tension would change the spline that the physics worker also evaluates, so they are not offered. Reverse, respace, subdivide and simplify cover direction and density.
